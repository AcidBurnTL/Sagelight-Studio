Winter Heating Costs in CEE: 2025–2026 Season Review

Four neighbouring countries, four very different heating seasons. Romania raised VAT on district heat and ended its classic gas price cap. Poland’s regulated gas tariff fell twice. Hungary kept its regulated household prices while the debate about their cost grew louder. Czechia saw small changes in the regulated part of the bill. Below is what changed, with sources, and what to check before winter 2026–2027.

Updated 13 October 2026. This review was rewritten to keep only figures we could trace to a source. Each one links to the list at the bottom of the page.

Romania: higher VAT on heat, a new gas mechanism

District heating. From 1 November 2025 the VAT rate on heat supplied to households rose from 5% to 11%, a reduced rate announced until 31 March 2026. In Bucharest that put the price billed to households at 366.94 lei per gigacalorie including VAT. Households pay 330 lei/Gcal before VAT while the real cost of the heat is about 1,000 lei; the city subsidises roughly two thirds. In August 2026 the press reported that a price rise for winter 2026–2027 is possible, with the city heat company Termoenergetica carrying debts of almost 2 billion lei.

Gas. Households had a final price capped at 0.31 lei/kWh under OUG 6/2025 until 31 March 2026. From 1 April 2026, OUG 12/2026 introduced a new mechanism valid until 31 March 2027: each supplier calculates the bill price from several components, and producers sell gas for household consumption at a regulated 110 lei/MWh. The energy minister said household bills would not exceed 0.31 lei/kWh.

Electricity, for context. Eurostat reports that Romanian household electricity prices rose 58.6% in national currency between the second half of 2024 and the second half of 2025, the largest increase in the EU. Romania’s household electricity price cap had ended on 30 June 2025.

Poland: regulated gas tariff cut twice

Household gas from the former PGNiG Obrót Detaliczny (now trading as myORLEN) is still sold under a tariff approved by the energy regulator, URE. From 1 July 2025 the tariff gas price fell by 14.8%. On 10 February 2026 URE approved a further cut to 197.29 zł/MWh from 25 February to 30 June 2026, about 3.4% lower, after wholesale prices on the Polish power exchange kept falling. Fixed subscription fees stayed the same, and distribution charges are set separately by the network operator.

District-heating prices in Poland are also set through tariffs approved by the URE president, case by case for each licensed heat company, usually for 12 months at a time.

Hungary: regulated prices up to average consumption

Under the rezsicsökkentés scheme, households pay a regulated price for gas and electricity up to an annual average consumption level, and a much higher, still government-set, price above it. Eurostat lists Hungary with the lowest household electricity price in the EU in the second half of 2025: €10.82 per 100 kWh, against an EU average of €28.96.

The scheme’s cost is now openly debated. An October 2026 opinion piece in Telex, citing a Nép­szava compilation, puts budget support for regulated energy prices at over 5,000 billion forints between 2022 and 2025, and lists reform ideas such as an extra consumption band and limiting the discount to one permanently occupied home. No change had been adopted at the time of writing.

Czechia: small moves in the regulated part of the bill

In Czechia the Energy Regulatory Office (ERÚ) sets the regulated components of the bill, such as distribution, while suppliers set the price of the energy itself. For 2026 ERÚ announced an average 1.1% rise in the regulated electricity component for low-voltage customers and a 4.7% rise for households’ gas, citing the need for network investment and, for gas, consumption at a thirty-year low. The supplier part of the price is where households can still compare and switch.

What to check before winter 2026–2027

  • Romania: whether the 11% VAT on heat is extended, your city’s gigacalorie price, and your gas price per kWh under OUG 12/2026.
  • Poland: the current URE-approved gas tariff and your heat company’s tariff decision.
  • Hungary: whether your consumption stays under the average band, and any reform of the scheme.
  • Czechia: your supplier’s energy price against current offers, since the regulated part moves little.
  • Everywhere: log your heating bill every month. Only then can you tell whether a rise came from the price or from your own consumption.
