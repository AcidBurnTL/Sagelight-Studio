Winter Heating Costs in CEE: 2025–2026 Season Review

The 2025–2026 heating season in Central and Eastern Europe was characterised by diverging price trajectories across countries that superficially look similar. Romania experienced moderate district heating price increases offset by a mild December. Poland saw sharp gas price spikes in November that eased by January. Hungary’s capped energy tariffs held for residential customers but created supply tension in the commercial sector. Czechia navigated the season with the most stable pricing of the four.

Romania

District heating (termoficare) prices in Bucharest increased 8.3% at the start of the season, following ANRE’s revised heat tariff methodology. The mild December meant average consumption was 12% below the five-year seasonal norm, partially offsetting the price increase. Net effect: most Bucharest households paid roughly €5–€15 more per month than in the 2024–2025 season. Gas customers on competitive market contracts fared better, with market prices in Q4 2025 actually 6% below the previous year due to high LNG storage levels across the EU.

Poland

Poland’s heating season was the most volatile of the four countries. PGNiG gas prices for household customers spiked in November due to a temporary imbalance in the Baltic connector flows, adding approximately PLN 120–180 (roughly €28–€42) to a typical month’s gas bill. By January, prices had normalised. Households on district heating (ciepłownia) were largely insulated from gas price volatility, as local heat tariffs are approved by the URE regulator and change at most once or twice a year.

Hungary

Hungary maintained its residential energy price caps through the season, meaning household gas and electricity prices remained significantly below EU market rates. The policy continues to cost the Hungarian government an estimated HUF 300–400 billion annually. For households, the direct financial impact was positive — bills stayed low — but the policy creates uncertainty about how quickly a cap removal could increase costs if implemented rapidly.

Czechia

Czechia had the steadiest season of the four. Most Czech homes heat with natural gas or district heating (dálkové vytápění), and household energy prices are overseen by the Energy Regulatory Office (ERÚ). Easing wholesale gas across the EU through 2025 fed into more stable retail offers, and district-heating tariffs — set locally and adjusted at most once or twice a year — changed little mid-season. As elsewhere, the households that benefited most were those who checked their contract date against current market rates rather than rolling over a tariff signed at the 2022 peak.

What This Means for 2026–2027

EU storage refill obligations will keep upward pressure on gas prices through summer 2026. Romania’s district heating operators are facing infrastructure investment pressure that typically flows through to tariff increases.
