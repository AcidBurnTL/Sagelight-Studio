Car documents checklist: what to carry, what to keep, and when it all expires

Short answer: every car on EU roads needs a registration certificate, compulsory third-party insurance, a valid roadworthiness test once it's old enough, and a driver with a valid licence; many countries add road tolls or vignettes. The EU sets minimum rules, but inspection intervals and fines are national, so the dates differ from country to country. Below is a checklist with the EU baseline, examples from Germany, France and Romania, and a simple way to keep every date in view.

Insurance: covered across the EU

When you register a car in any EU country you must insure it for third-party liability, and that compulsory cover is valid in all other EU countries. The EU's Your Europe portal also notes that your number plate is proof that you have liability insurance, so police abroad won't normally stop you just to check it. It still recommends taking your insurance papers with you, because they are the easiest way to prove cover after an accident.

Optional cover (for theft or damage to your own car) is a different matter: there are no EU-wide rules, so check your policy before a trip.

Roadworthiness tests: the EU minimum and three national schedules

EU Directive 2014/45 sets the minimum: passenger cars must be tested four years after first registration and then every two years. Countries may test more often, and many do.

The trap is that nobody reminds you. France's official guide, for example, states plainly that no notice is sent: the test is up to you. The certificate shows the next due date, so record it somewhere you'll see it.

Driving licence: valid everywhere in the EU, but it does expire

A licence issued in one EU country can be used anywhere in the EU. Under the current rules, car licences have an administrative validity of 10 years, and countries may choose up to 15. Romania, for instance, sets 10 years from the date of issue.

That is changing. In October 2025 the European Parliament approved new rules under which car and motorcycle licences will be valid for 15 years (countries may keep 10 years where the licence doubles as a national ID), alongside a digital licence on your phone. Countries have three years to transpose the rules and another year to implement them, so check your national authority before assuming your licence has more time.

One more detail worth knowing: a driving licence is not a travel document. The EU says licences may be refused as proof of identity, so carry your ID card or passport when you travel.

Vignettes and tolls

Several countries charge for road use through a time-based vignette or an electronic toll account linked to your number plate. Romania is a good example of how these systems change: since 1 October 2026 its rovinietă and the new TollRo charges are sold only through official channels such as the etoll.ro portal and the TollRo app, and a plain bank transfer to the road agency doesn't count as payment. Before driving abroad, check the official site of each country you'll cross.

What to keep at home, in the car, and on your phone

  • In the car or on you: whatever local law requires you to show at a roadside check. The list differs by country, so check the rules for each country you drive in.
  • At home: the purchase contract, the vehicle identity papers that aren't needed on the road, service and repair invoices, and old inspection reports. They matter when you sell the car or dispute a repair.
  • On your phone: encrypted copies of everything, plus every expiry date. A copy won't replace an original at a police check, but it makes a lost document far easier to replace and keeps the dates in front of you.

Buying a used car: check the paperwork before you pay

A used car's documents tell you as much as a test drive. Before you hand over money:

  • Match the identification number on the registration certificate with the one on the car itself.
  • Look at the inspection history, not just the current certificate. A string of failed tests, or a mileage that goes down between inspections, is a warning sign.
  • Check the insurance status where your country offers an official lookup. In Romania, for example, the AIDA database lets anyone check whether a car has a valid RCA policy by its registration number.
  • Ask for the service and repair invoices, and keep them with your purchase contract. You'll want them again when you sell.
  • Note every new date on the day you buy: when the seller's insurance ends, when the next inspection is due, and any vignette that came with the car.

Driving abroad: what changes

Your EU licence and your compulsory insurance travel with you, but local rules don't stop at the border. Equipment requirements (warning triangles, high-visibility vests, first-aid kits), low-emission zones and toll systems are national. Before a road trip, spend ten minutes on the official site of each country you'll drive through, and keep your insurance papers in the car: the EU says they're the easiest way to prove you're covered after an accident.

Never miss a date: a simple routine

  1. Write down four dates per car: insurance end, next inspection, vignette end, and the licence expiry for each driver.
  2. Set two reminders for each: one a month before (time to compare insurance or book an inspection slot) and one a week before.
  3. When you renew, scan the new document straight away and replace the old date.

FAQ

How often does a car need a roadworthiness test in the EU?

The EU minimum is four years after first registration and then every two years, but countries can test more often. Germany tests new cars after 36 months and then every 24 months; Romania after three years, then every two years, and yearly once a car is 12 years old.

Do I need to carry my insurance certificate when driving in another EU country?

Your number plate counts as proof of liability insurance in the EU, so it isn't normally checked at the roadside. The EU still recommends carrying your insurance papers because they make it easier to prove cover after an accident.

How long is a driving licence valid in the EU?

Under the current rules, car licences are valid for 10 years, and countries may choose up to 15. New EU rules approved in 2025 make 15 years the standard once countries transpose them.

Can I show a photo of my documents instead of the originals?

Generally no. Roadside checks follow national rules, and a photo is not a substitute where the law requires the original. Digital copies are for your records and for replacing lost documents.
